Where Americans move, and what it costs

By Zoe Rowe · Financial Data Analytics

Every year the Census Bureau asks where people lived a year ago, and every year a few million Americans give a different state than the one they live in now. This report follows those movers across two decades (2005–2024) of state-to-state migration data, paired with Zillow's home value index for every state they left and every state they landed in. The pattern is consistent: people are leaving expensive, slow-growing states in the Northeast and Midwest for cheaper, faster-growing states in the South and West — and the price gap between where they came from and where they're headed has only widened since 2020.

1. Interstate migration has been remarkably stable for twenty years

Roughly 7.5 million people move from one U.S. state to another every year — about 2% of the population. The low point was 6.83 million in 2010, in the aftermath of the housing crash, when moving was expensive and jobs were scarce everywhere. The high point was 8.30 million in 2022, as remote work loosened the tie between where people lived and where they worked. Even across a financial crisis, a pandemic, and a housing boom, the number has never strayed more than 15% from its 19-year average.

2. The South is absorbing the Northeast and Midwest's population

Add up net migration — people arriving minus people leaving — by region for every year since 2005, and the direction never changes. The South has gained a net 8.7 million people from the rest of the country over that span; the Northeast has lost 5.0 million and the Midwest 2.5 million. The West used to be a net gainer too, but has been a net loser every year since 2021, shedding 153,000 people in 2024 alone — a sign that high-cost West Coast states are now sending more people to the South than they receive from the Northeast.

Net migration by Census region, 2005–2024

3. Florida, Texas, and the Carolinas are the biggest winners; California and New York the biggest losers

Averaged over the five most recent years (2020–2024), Florida gained a net 665,000 people and Texas gained 532,000 — more than the next several gainers combined. On the other side, California lost a net 1.27 million people and New York lost 840,000, roughly double what any other state lost. These aren't small swings: California's five-year net loss is close to the entire population of New Hampshire.

Net migration, 5-year average (2020–2024)

4. California to Texas is the single busiest route in the country

In 2024, an estimated 77,161 people moved from California to Texas — the largest flow between any two states, and nearly 30,000 more than the reverse trip (Texas to California, 45,447 people). Four of the eight busiest routes in the country start in California, landing in Texas, Nevada, and Arizona — all cheaper housing markets one state or one desert away.

5. Most movers are chasing lower housing costs

Weight every move by how many people made it, and 53% of movers in 2024 relocated to a state with a lower home value index than the one they left. That share has held between 52% and 55% every year since 2005 — movers have consistently, if narrowly, favored affordability over the two decades on record, in good economies and bad.

Share of movers headed to a cheaper state

6. The price gap between origin and destination has nearly doubled since 2019

It's not just that people move to cheaper states — the size of the gap has grown. Weighting by the number of movers, the average home-value gap between where people came from and where they landed was −$10,895 in 2019. By 2021 it had jumped to −$24,899 as remote work let people leave expensive coastal metros for good, and it remains a wide −$18,321 in 2024. Movers aren't just picking cheaper states — they're picking states with a bigger price difference than before the pandemic.

Movers-weighted home value gap (destination − origin)

7. Home values surged hardest in the same states that are gaining people

Since 2005, home values are up 191% in Idaho, 178% in Utah, and 174% in Montana — three of the biggest net-migration winners on record. Fast in-migration and fast price growth go together: buyers moving in from pricier states can outbid local wages. At the other end, Illinois (+37%), Nevada (+41%) and Connecticut (+41%) had the slowest price growth of any state with usable data, alongside some of the weakest net migration.

8. Puerto Rico's outflow spiked the year after Hurricane Maria

Hurricane Maria made landfall in September 2017. In the 2018 survey year that followed, 133,451 people left Puerto Rico for the mainland — up 37% from 97,488 the year before, and the highest outflow anywhere in the 19-year record. Outflow fell back to pre-storm levels within two years, a reminder that this panel captures real, dateable events, not just slow demographic drift.

About this data

Migration flows come from the U.S. Census Bureau's State-to-State Migration Flows tables, built from the American Community Survey's 1-year estimates, 2005–2024 (the ACS did not release 1-year estimates for 2020, so that year is absent from every chart). One row is the estimated number of people who lived in one state or Puerto Rico ("prior state") one year before the survey and a different state or Puerto Rico ("current state") at the time of the survey. Movers who stayed in the same state are dropped, since this report is about migration between states, not within them.

Cost of living is proxied by the Zillow Home Value Index, state level, averaged from monthly to annual figures. It is joined onto the migration data by state and year, separately for the origin and destination state of each move. Zillow does not publish a state-level index for North Dakota or Puerto Rico, so the small number of rows involving those two are excluded from every cost-of-living calculation (about 2.3% of rows).

Every "net migration" figure is movers into a state − movers out of that state, summed across all of that state's partner states for the year (or years) in question. Every "share who moved to a cheaper state" figure weights each state-pair's home-value gap by the number of movers who made that move, so a large flow between two states counts more than a small one. All numbers are reproducible from data/processed/state_migration_cost_panel.csv via the scripts in data/ — see the repository for the full pipeline.

Data: U.S. Census Bureau (ACS State-to-State Migration Flows) & Zillow Research (ZHVI). Built with plain HTML, CSS, and Chart.js.
